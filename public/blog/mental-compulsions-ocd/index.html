Mental Compulsions in OCD: The Rituals You Cannot See

By Theekshitha Vadladi, CCC-Q · November 3, 2026

Someone can look still while spending hours reviewing, checking or trying to undo a thought. Understanding the purpose of that thinking helps explain mental compulsions.

General information to support a conversation about care. This article does not diagnose your experience or provide an individual treatment plan.

What is a mental compulsion?

A mental compulsion is a repeated internal act performed in response to an obsession or a rule someone feels driven to follow. It may aim to reduce distress, prevent a feared outcome or reach a feeling of certainty. The ritual happens in thought, so it can be difficult for other people to notice.

In OCD, a person may repeatedly reconstruct a conversation, count silently, replace an unwanted thought with another thought, or check whether a feeling is present. What matters is the role the response plays in the pattern. Remembering an event or repeating a phrase is not automatically a compulsion.

Sources: International OCD Foundation: What is OCD?; NIMH: Obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Examples of rituals that happen internally

Consider someone who remembers a conversation and worries that a comment revealed something terrible about them. They replay the wording, inspect their intentions and search their memory for a detail that would settle the concern. An answer briefly feels convincing, until another possible interpretation appears.

That example illustrates a possible OCD cycle, rather than a way to identify OCD from one situation. Internal responses can take different forms, including:

  • Mental reviewing: replaying events or conversations to resolve doubt.
  • Neutralising: using a thought, image or phrase to try to cancel another one.
  • Internal checking: repeatedly inspecting feelings, intentions or bodily reactions.
  • Counting or repeating: following an internal sequence until a rule or feeling of completeness is met.

Sources: International OCD Foundation: What is OCD?; IOCDF: Understanding compulsive reasoning and mental reviewing.

Why the answer may never feel finished

A ritual may bring short-lived relief. The problem is that the next doubt can call for another round, and the demand for certainty grows harder to satisfy. Time and attention become organised around answering the concern instead of taking part in the day.

This helps explain why more reasoning does not always feel useful. The issue may be the repeated attempt to obtain certainty, rather than a missing piece of information. Compulsive reviewing can also occur after a visible ritual or an exposure, so the full sequence deserves attention.

Sources: International OCD Foundation: What is OCD?; IOCDF: Understanding compulsive reasoning and mental reviewing.

Ordinary reflection and valued practices need context

People think carefully about mistakes, reflect on relationships and use repeated words in meaningful ways. Prayer, for example, can be an important religious practice. Its form alone cannot tell us whether a compulsive process is involved.

A useful clinical conversation considers the purpose, rigidity, distress and effect on life. Faith and cultural commitments deserve respectful understanding. It also matters whether trying to classify every thought as a ritual has itself become another time-consuming search for certainty.

Sources: International OCD Foundation: What is OCD?; IOCDF: OCD and scrupulosity.

How ERP includes mental rituals

Exposure and Response Prevention is a form of cognitive behavioural therapy used for OCD. Exposure involves planned contact with a trigger; response prevention addresses the ritual that usually follows. When the ritual is mental, that internal response belongs in the plan.

Response prevention does not require an empty mind. A thought appearing and the effort to neutralise or repeatedly resolve it are different parts of the experience. Understanding that distinction helps identify what is being practised. The starting point and pace need to be planned for the individual.

At Unravel, ACT is the main framework and ERP is used for OCD. ACT supports willingness, attention and action guided by values alongside the OCD-specific work. The purpose is connected with the life the rituals are interrupting.

Sources: IOCDF: Exposure and Response Prevention; ACBS: The six core processes of ACT.

What to bring to a first conversation

A broad description is enough to begin: what tends to trigger the loop, what you feel driven to do mentally and how much it interrupts your day. Questions about training, mental rituals and the proposed approach can help you consider whether a service is appropriate.
