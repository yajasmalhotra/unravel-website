UNDERSTANDING OCD

OCD types, themes and intrusive thoughts.

OCD can focus on contamination, harm, relationships, faith or a memory that feels impossible to leave alone. The subject may change while the cycle of intrusive thoughts and compulsive responses remains familiar.

LANGUAGE FOR THE EXPERIENCE

What do “types of OCD” actually describe?

When people search for “types of OCD”, they often mean the subjects their obsessions focus on: contamination, responsibility, relationships, faith or another area. These names are ways of describing presentations of OCD. They do not make every concern a separate diagnosis, and this list is not a diagnostic test.

More than one theme can be present, and the focus can change. Understanding care also means looking at the responses to a concern: what you feel driven to do, the relief you seek and the effect on your day. NIMH describes OCD, including changes in symptoms and the role of assessment.

You do not need to find a perfect category before asking about support. The OCD counselling page explains Unravel’s care pathway, including the online format, ERP and practical details.

COMMON THEMES

Common OCD themes.

The examples below describe common presentations. Themes can overlap, and one person’s focus can change over time. Matching an example is not enough to establish OCD; distress, compulsions, context and the effect on life all matter.

Contamination OCD

Contamination concerns can lead to repeated washing, cleaning, checking or avoidance. The feared contamination may involve germs, substances or a persistent feeling of being unclean. Care considers what the response is trying to achieve and how much it interrupts life, alongside genuine health guidance.

NIMH: OCD signs and symptoms

Just-right OCD and incompleteness

A sense that something is unfinished or not quite right can lead to repeating, arranging or redoing. Sometimes the distress is tension or incompleteness rather than a clearly named feared event. This differs from simply preferring something neat or well made.

IOCDF: Just-right OCD fact sheet (PDF)

Checking and responsibility

Doubt about a mistake or a possible consequence can be followed by repeated checking, reviewing or reassurance-seeking. The pattern may interrupt leaving home, completing a task or returning to another activity. A careful habit alone does not establish OCD.

IOCDF: Understanding obsessions and compulsions

Harm-related intrusive thoughts

Unwanted thoughts, images or urges about harm can become the focus of OCD. Responses may include checking, avoiding reminders, inspecting intentions or seeking reassurance. The thought’s subject alone cannot establish a diagnosis or determine risk; understanding the whole experience requires individual assessment.

NIMH: Understanding OCD

Relationship OCD (ROCD)

Relationship-focused OCD can involve repeated testing of feelings, comparisons or reassurance about a partner or relationship. Ordinary relationship doubts also occur. The pattern is understood through the function, repetition and cost of the responses, rather than the presence of doubt alone.

IOCDF: Relationship OCD

Scrupulosity: faith and morality

Scrupulosity describes OCD-related religious or moral concerns. Repeated attempts to be certain about wrongdoing may include reassurance, confession or mental rituals. Faith and valued religious practices deserve respectful understanding; being committed to a belief is not itself a symptom.

IOCDF: OCD and scrupulosity

Doubt about memories or past events

Labels such as real-event or false-memory OCD describe concerns about what happened and attempts to resolve the doubt through repeated reviewing. The label cannot establish whether a particular memory is accurate. Care considers the pattern without promising certainty about the event.

IOCDF: Clinical presentation on real-event and false-memory concerns (PDF)

THE LESS VISIBLE RESPONSES

Mental compulsions and the phrase “pure O”.

A person may replay conversations, check their feelings, repeat a phrase or try to cancel one thought with another. These can function as mental compulsions when they respond to an obsession or a rule the person feels driven to follow. Someone can look still while these rituals occupy hours. NIMH includes internal rituals in its description of OCD.

“Pure O” is sometimes used informally when obsessions are prominent and visible rituals are less apparent. The phrase can obscure mental reviewing, reassurance-seeking and other compulsions that still need attention. The IOCDF’s guide to compulsive reasoning explains why mental responses deserve attention.

The purpose of a response matters. Reflection, prayer or checking a real responsibility is not automatically compulsive. If the effort to classify every thought has become another repeated search for certainty, that is useful to mention in a clinical conversation.

FINDING SUPPORT

ERP addresses responses across OCD themes.

Exposure and Response Prevention is a first-line psychological treatment for OCD. It involves agreed practice with triggers and compulsive responses. The IOCDF treatment guide describes ERP and other care options.

At Unravel, ACT is the main approach, with ERP used in OCD counselling. ACT brings attention to values and psychological flexibility. Your culture, beliefs, responsibilities and goals belong in the discussion of a plan.

COMMON QUESTIONS

Questions about the labels.

Do I have to fit one theme?

No. The terms describe common areas of concern, and experiences can overlap or change. You can ask about support without choosing a category first.

Are unwanted thoughts enough to establish OCD?

An unwanted thought alone does not establish OCD. The wider pattern, distress, repeated responses and effect on life are relevant to a clinical assessment.

Does treatment differ for each OCD theme?

The subject and circumstances help shape a plan, while ERP addresses the compulsive cycle across themes. Unravel offers individual OCD counselling, with practice planned around the person’s needs rather than a separate service for each label.
